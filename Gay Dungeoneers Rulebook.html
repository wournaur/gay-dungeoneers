Introduction

Gay Dungeoneers is a homebrew system that splices Dungeons & Dragons with Cortex Prime, aiming for a middle ground between the two.

D&D and Cortex Prime each solve a different problem. D&D delivers satisfying tactical combat, but narration and mechanics stay separate — however creatively you describe a sword swing, it resolves the same way. Cortex does the opposite: your narration sets the traits in your dice pool, so how you describe an action directly shapes how it's resolved.

The tradeoff is that Cortex abstracts combat. That suits fast, cinematic conflicts, but the tactical depth and tension our group wants has to be rebuilt through custom SFX and optional mods — bolted on rather than built in.

Gay Dungeoneers keeps Cortex's narrate-first philosophy but pairs it with a D&D-style combat framework: narration shapes mechanics, while rolling for damage, landing crits, and grinding down an opponent still matter. It's not an improvement on either system — both do what they're designed to do well — just an experiment in combining what we liked most from each.

The World

This setting revolves around dungeons. Their depths promise wealth, power, and discovery, drawing countless people to make a living exploring them. Whether your character is in it for fortune, fame, knowledge, or something more personal is a story for you and your DM to tell.

Character Building

This system is built around the idea of a tabletop roguelike. Rather than following a traditional class progression, characters begin with only a broad combat role — Damage, Tank, or Support. The rest of their build develops organically through the talents they acquire during each dungeon run, allowing the same character to play differently from one expedition to the next.

Combat is therefore the primary focus of the system. Each run is designed to present new combinations of talents and new tactical decisions, encouraging experimentation over optimization. Progress earned inside a dungeon is temporary: talents and levels are lost upon escaping or dying. Lasting progression comes from the world outside the dungeon — building relationships, acquiring equipment, and improving skills during downtime. A compelling setting between expeditions is therefore just as important as the dungeon itself.

Playing This System

This system wasn't designed to be run purely with paper and dice — it started life as an Avrae alias collection, and that's still the intended way to play it: Gay Dungeoneers Alias Collection.

A fair amount of what's in this book leans on math the bot is quietly handling behind the scenes — critical tier scaling, success tier scaling, status effect stacking, and more. None of it is impossible to run by hand, but it adds up fast at the table. Unless you're up for tweaking the system to make manual play smoother, running it through the alias collection is heavily recommended.

1. Character Creation

Background Bonuses

Every character starts with three background bonuses, each a flat +1: Race, Occupation, and Affiliation. Race and Occupation follow directly from your concept. Affiliation takes more thought — work with your DM to settle on the faction, group, belief, or bond your character is tied to.

Stats

Every character is built on three stats: Physical, Mental, and Social.

  • Physical — Your body in action: strength, endurance, dexterity, raw physical capability.
  • Mental — Your mind in action: intellect, willpower, magical aptitude, cunning.
  • Social — Your presence in action: charisma, empathy, force of personality.

Skills

Skills sit underneath those stats, but they aren't locked to one — any skill can pair with whichever stat best fits how you're narrating the action.

  • Cast — Channeling and shaping magic into an effect.
  • Endure — Withstanding pain, exhaustion, disease, or hardship.
  • Fight — Striking something up close, armed or unarmed.
  • Finesse — Fine, deliberate manual precision: lockpicking, forgery, delicate craftsmanship.
  • Focus — Concentration and mental discipline under pressure.
  • Influence — Persuading, inspiring, or pressuring someone through honest appeal.
  • Know — Recalling or reasoning out information you've learned.
  • Labor — Sustained physical exertion: hauling, digging, building, breaking.
  • Move — Speed, agility, and reflexes in motion.
  • Notice — Picking up on details others miss.
  • Perform — Entertaining or holding an audience's attention.
  • Shoot — Striking something at range with a projectile, physical or magical.
  • Sneak — Moving or acting without being detected.
  • Survive — Reading and enduring the wilds: tracking, foraging, weathering the elements.
  • Throw — Hurling an object accurately.
  • Tinker — Building, repairing, or fiddling with mechanisms and devices.
  • Treat — Tending wounds and ailments, mundane or magical.
  • Trick — Deceiving, misdirecting, or talking your way around the truth.

Assigning Points

You get either two +3s or one +4 to assign across your stats and skills, in any combination. Anything you don't raise defaults to +0, though you can also bump individual stats or skills to +1 or +2 without touching your +3s/+4.

Negatives use the same table in reverse and are optional. A flaw or two adds more character than a sheet of pure strengths. If unsure how far to take it, cap it at -3.

Extra Bonuses

You have 4 points to spend on extra bonuses — niche specialties not covered by a stat or skill. You might have +0 Shoot overall but exceptional skill with a bow specifically, so all 4 points go into +4 Archery. These don't have to be combat-related: spotting a halfling from a gnome at a glance, or flawless calligraphy, both qualify.

How far your points go depends on specificity. A general bonus — cooking, drawing — caps at +2 unless you can justify +4 to your DM. A genuinely niche one — stir-frying specifically, drawing girls specifically — needs no justification; put all 4 points in if you want.

Weaknesses

Same idea as negative stats and skills: cap at -3 if unsure. Unlike stats and skills, there's no limit on how many weaknesses you can take — vices, allergies, phobias, whatever fits your character.

2. Core Mechanics

Building a Pool

Every roll needs at least one stat and one skill. Beyond that, background bonuses, extra bonuses, item bonuses, and even weaknesses can fold into your pool if your narration justifies it. Nothing is added automatically — if you can't make a case for it, it doesn't apply.

The DM decides whether a pairing or bonus fits your description.

Meets It Beats It

Nearly everything resolves the same way: roll your pool (d20 + modifiers) against a target number. Equal or higher succeeds. This applies to attacking, creating an advantage, or resisting — only what a success does changes, covered action by action below.

3. Initiative

Roll a d20 + Physical + Move. Highest total acts first.

Groups (see Party, Groups & Roles) roll together instead of individually: average every member's Physical + Move, and use that as the modifier for one d20 roll representing the group.

Initiative rerolls for everyone at the start of every round, so turn order changes each round.

4. Turns & Action Points

Every combatant has 3 Action Points (AP), refreshing at the start of each round, not at the start of your turn. AP spent reacting earlier in the round is AP you won't have on your own turn, and vice versa.

There are five actions: Attack, Create an Advantage, Help, Move, and React.

With one exception, each action costs AP equal to how many actions you've already taken this round, counted across all action types combined. Your first action costs 1 AP, your second costs 2 AP, your third would cost 3 AP — since you only have 3 AP total, two actions per round is the practical ceiling.

This counter is shared: Attack, then Help, then Create an Advantage still costs 1, then 2, then 3, regardless of which actions you mix.

React is the exception — it never escalates and doesn't count toward the shared counter. It has its own flat cost, covered below.

5. The Actions

Attack

The attacker narrates their action first, which determines their pool (stat, skill, extra bonuses). The defender then narrates their reaction and builds their own pool. The defender's total becomes the DC the attacker must meet or beat.

Meeting or beating that number is a hit; falling short deals no damage.

On a hit, check the margin. Every full +10 of margin grants one critical tier, uncapped.

Each critical tier multiplies your damage dice: total dice = base dice × (critical tiers + 1). A base 2d10 attack becomes 4d10 at one tier, 6d10 at two, and so on.

Create an Advantage

Narrate what you're trying to create — a bonus for yourself or an ally, or a weakness on an enemy. The DM sets a DC based on the difficulty. Meet or beat it to succeed.

Success grants at least 1 success tier for clearing the DC, plus one more per full +4 of margin, uncapped. The tier is the strength of what you made — a roll of 12 against a DC of 8 (margin 4) creates something at +2, whether that's a +2 bonus for an ally or a -2 weakness on an enemy.

Help

Narrate how you're helping, then add your relevant skill modifier as a bonus to an ally's roll. Multiple allies can help the same person and those bonuses stack, but the same helper can't help the same target twice on the same roll.

Move

Move a number of tiles equal to (Physical + Move) ÷ 2, rounded down, minimum 1.

React

Reactions respond to another combatant's action and are the one part of the action economy that doesn't escalate.

Defend. Build a defense pool against an incoming attack as normal. Costs 1 AP flat — consecutive defends never cost more.

Out of AP when you need to defend? You become Overwhelmed: you can still defend, but take a cumulative -2 penalty for each consecutive attack you defend against with no AP left.

Concede. Let the attack land outright instead of defending. It auto-hits but costs no AP. Since there's no DC to beat, there's no margin — the hit can never crit. Against a small attack, conceding to bank AP is often the smarter play.

Intercept. Take a hit meant for an ally. You roll your own defense pool, built around how you narrate the intercept — blocking with a shield might be Physical + Endure — and the attacker's roll is measured against your total instead of your ally's. Only you roll, and only you spend AP.

Required proximity depends on the narration: shoving in with a shield means being adjacent; deflecting an arrow from across the room doesn't. The DM judges plausibility.

A failed intercept also depends on the narration. Up close — blocking with a shield — a failed roll means the attack hits you instead. At range — knocking a projectile out of the air — a failed roll means the attack still finds your ally.

Costs 2 AP flat (base React cost plus a 1 AP surcharge); consecutive intercepts still cost 2 AP.

Counterattack. Roll an attack back against your attacker instead of defending. Whoever rolls higher lands the hit; the loser takes the winner's damage. Ties go to the original attacker. Costs 2 AP flat, no escalation on repeats.

Help (reactive). Same rules as Help above, used as a reaction. Costs 2 AP flat, no escalation on repeats.

6. Spellcasting

Every spell requires Channeling first: use a Create an Advantage action targeting yourself against the spell's listed DC — your success tier becomes your Channeling bonus. Some spells need a minimum amount banked before you can cast them.

Once you have enough, spend it on a later action to cast the spell.

7. Weaknesses & Status Effects

A debilitating hit — burning you, restraining you, knocking you off balance — applies a weakness to your defense pool. That weakness has a name (its "parent," like Burning or Concussed) and a magnitude, and carries an attached status effect describing what happens each round. Most status effects scale off that magnitude — the parent modifier.

The full list of status effects is in the Glossary, alongside the talent list.

8. Party, Groups & Roles

Combatants can form groups of up to 3. Grouping affects two things:

  • Initiative — the group rolls once together, using the averaged Physical + Move modifier described in Initiative.
  • Hit points — the group shares one combined HP pool. If that pool hits 0, everyone in the group dies together.

That shared fate is why who you group with — and what role each person plays — matters before a run starts.

There are three roles: Damage, Tank, and Support.

9. Talents & Leveling

At the start of a dungeon run, you pick a role. This determines your talent pool: your role's talents plus General. Skip a role and the DM pools Damage, Tank, Support, and General together instead. Either way, you're offered 3 options.

Outside the dungeon, your character sits at Level 0 — no need to track hit points or mana during downtime. The system isn't built for combat outside the dungeon; making that work is homebrew territory.

Entering a dungeon grants your first level. After that, leveling pace is up to your table — we default to a level every 3 rooms cleared, as a suggested pace rather than a rule. Each level grants:

  • 1d10 hit points (minimum 5)
  • 1d8 mana (minimum 4)
  • One more talent, offered as a fresh set of 3 options

Talent picks aren't locked to your starting role — pick from Tank at the start, then pull from Damage on a later level-up if you want. Every level-up re-offers options from whichever pools you choose.

10. Dungeon Runs

A run is structured in rooms. Every 10th room is a boss fight.

Defeating the boss grants your whole group a random-sized wishbox.

If your group's shared HP pool hits 0, that's permadeath — roll new characters.

If you don't think you can survive the next room, you can escape instead. That's always an option, but it wipes your progress entirely: level resets to 1, and you lose every talent from the run.

The only progression that survives between runs happens during downtime sessions outside the dungeon, where you can learn new skills or abilities, or buy items for your next attempt.
